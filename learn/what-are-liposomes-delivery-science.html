What are liposomes? The 60-year-old delivery science behind the armor

A liposome is a microscopic bubble made of the same material as your cell membranes. It is one of the most studied delivery systems in medicine, and it is the reason The Metabolic can carry a fragile botanical past your stomach acid.

Reviewed by the Prickless Bio Science Team Updated August 2026 6 min read

If you have shopped for a supplement lately, you have seen the word liposomal on the label of everything from vitamin C to curcumin to berberine. It sounds like marketing. It is actually one of the oldest and best-validated ideas in drug delivery. Here is what a liposome really is, where the science came from, and why the carrier can matter as much as the ingredient inside it.

Key takeaways

  • A liposome is a phospholipid bubble, made from the same class of lipid as your cell membranes.
  • The structure was described in 1965 by British scientist Alec Bangham and has been studied for delivery ever since.
  • A liposome can shield a fragile molecule from stomach acid and enzymes and improve how much reaches the bloodstream.
  • The same delivery family powers approved cancer medicines and the lipid nanoparticles used in modern vaccines.
  • In The Metabolic, a sunflower phospholipid bilayer carries berberine, a compound that is otherwise very poorly absorbed.

What exactly is a liposome?

A liposome is a tiny spherical vesicle, usually between 50 and 500 nanometers across, built from a phospholipid bilayer. Phospholipids are unusual molecules: each has a water-loving head and two water-fearing tails. Drop enough of them into water and they arrange themselves automatically, tails tucked away from the water and heads facing out, into a closed double-walled shell with a watery pocket inside.

That shape is the whole trick. The watery core can hold a water-soluble cargo, the oily bilayer can hold a fat-soluble one, and the outer surface is chemically identical to the membranes your own cells are wrapped in. A liposome is, in effect, a delivery capsule your body already recognizes.

A short history: from a 1965 discovery to modern medicine

The story starts with a British biophysicist named Alec Bangham. In the early 1960s, while studying blood clotting at the Babraham Institute near Cambridge, he mixed dried phospholipids with water under an electron microscope and watched them spontaneously form closed vesicles. His 1965 paper documenting these structures is now considered the birth of liposome science.

The moment scientists realized these bubbles could carry a cargo, the liposome stopped being a curiosity and became a delivery system.

The decades that followed turned the idea into real medicine:

  • 1995: Doxil, a liposomal form of the chemotherapy drug doxorubicin, became the first FDA-approved nanomedicine, using the shell to change how and where the drug traveled in the body.
  • Antifungal therapy: liposomal amphotericin B let doctors deliver a notoriously harsh drug with far less toxicity.
  • 2020 and 2021: the lipid nanoparticles that delivered mRNA vaccines to hundreds of millions of people are a direct descendant of the same phospholipid chemistry.

The point is simple. Liposomal delivery is not a fringe wellness trend. It is one of the most thoroughly validated delivery platforms in pharmaceutical history.

Why does the gut destroy so many supplements?

To understand why the shell matters, you have to appreciate how hostile the digestive tract is to a fragile molecule. Between the pill and your bloodstream, an ingredient has to survive:

  • Stomach acid strong enough to sit near a pH of 2.
  • A wave of digestive enzymes built to break molecules apart.
  • Efflux pumps in the gut wall, such as P-glycoprotein, that actively push certain compounds back out of the cells trying to absorb them.
  • First-pass metabolism, where the liver chemically alters much of what does get through before it ever reaches general circulation.

For a lot of promising compounds, the result is that only a tiny fraction of the dose you swallow ever makes it into your blood. That fraction has a name: bioavailability. And for some botanicals it is shockingly low.

How does a liposome get the cargo through?

A liposome addresses the gauntlet above in a few ways at once. First, it is a physical shield: the bilayer keeps acid and enzymes away from the cargo tucked inside. Second, because the shell is made of the same material as your cell membranes, intestinal cells tend to take it up readily rather than reject it. Third, some liposomal cargo appears to travel partly through the lymphatic system, sidestepping a share of that first-pass liver metabolism.

Add it up and more of the active ingredient can arrive intact and in circulation, which is the entire goal.

Does liposomal delivery actually improve absorption? The honest answer

Yes, for the right compounds, with real evidence behind it. Controlled research on liposomal vitamin C, for example, has shown higher blood levels than the same dose of standard vitamin C. Similar delivery strategies have improved the absorption of stubbornly fat-loving compounds like curcumin.

The honest caveat

The word liposomal on a label is only as good as the formulation behind it. It is a manufacturing claim, not a tightly regulated standard, and product quality varies enormously. A true, stable liposome that survives shelf life and stomach acid is very different from a splash of lecithin added for marketing. The principle is proven. The execution is where good products separate themselves.

Frequently asked questions

Are liposomes safe?
Liposomes are built from phospholipids, the same class of fat that makes up your own cell membranes. They have a long safety record in both medicine and nutrition. As with any supplement, check with your healthcare provider before starting.
Are liposomal supplements better than regular capsules?
For compounds that are poorly absorbed on their own, liposomal delivery can meaningfully increase how much reaches the bloodstream. It is not automatically better for every nutrient, and the benefit depends heavily on the quality of the formulation.
What is the difference between a liposome and a lipid nanoparticle?
They are close relatives. A classic liposome is a hollow phospholipid bilayer sphere with a watery core. A lipid nanoparticle is a newer, denser structure used to carry cargo like mRNA. Both rely on the same self-assembling phospholipid chemistry.
