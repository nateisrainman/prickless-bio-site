Berberine

Does liposomal berberine actually work? The bioavailability problem, explained

Berberine has some of the most interesting metabolic research of any plant compound. It also has a serious flaw: your body barely absorbs it. Understanding that flaw is the key to understanding why delivery matters.

Reviewed by the Prickless Bio Science Team Updated August 2026 7 min read

Search berberine and you will find two very different stories. One is a wall of enthusiastic research on metabolism and blood sugar. The other, quieter one is a pharmacology problem: berberine is one of the most poorly absorbed compounds people take on purpose. Both stories are true, and the gap between them is exactly where formulation earns its keep.

Key takeaways

  • Berberine is well studied for metabolism but notoriously difficult to absorb.
  • Its oral bioavailability is often reported below 1 percent in pharmacokinetic research.
  • Three culprits: low solubility, a P-glycoprotein pump that ejects it, and heavy first-pass metabolism.
  • Phospholipid and liposomal delivery are among the strategies studied to raise berberine absorption.
  • Encapsulation can also reduce the digestive discomfort some people get from raw berberine.

What is berberine, briefly?

Berberine is a bright yellow isoquinoline alkaloid found in several plants, including barberry, goldthread (Coptis chinensis), goldenseal, and Oregon grape. It has been used in traditional Chinese and Ayurvedic practice for centuries, and modern research took a sharp interest in it after a landmark 2006 study showed it activates AMPK, a central regulator of cellular metabolism. We cover that story in a companion article on berberine and AMPK.

The catch: berberine is barely absorbed

Here is the number that reframes everything. In pharmacokinetic studies, the oral bioavailability of berberine is frequently reported at under 1 percent. In plain terms, you can swallow a substantial dose and have the overwhelming majority of it never reach your bloodstream in usable form. It is a little like buying a full tank of gas and watching most of it leak onto the driveway.

Why is berberine so hard to absorb? Three culprits

  1. Low solubility and permeability. Berberine does not dissolve or cross the gut lining easily, so a lot of it simply passes through.
  2. P-glycoprotein efflux. The cells of your gut wall carry a pump called P-glycoprotein that grabs certain compounds and pushes them right back out into the intestine. Berberine is a known target, so the little that gets in often gets ejected before it can be absorbed.
  3. First-pass metabolism. Whatever survives is heavily processed by the gut wall and liver before reaching general circulation, further shrinking the amount that arrives intact.

Stack these together and you get low, and often inconsistent, blood levels from standard berberine capsules.

How do you fix a bioavailability problem?

This is a well-worn problem in pharmaceutical science, and there is a toolkit for it: reducing particle size, pairing a compound with natural absorption enhancers, and, most relevant here, complexing or encapsulating it in phospholipids. The goal of every one of these is the same, to increase the fraction of the dose that actually makes it into the blood.

Where liposomal and phospholipid delivery come in

Wrapping berberine in a phospholipid shell does several helpful things at once. It makes the package more compatible with the fatty membranes of your gut cells, it shields the cargo from the acidic, enzyme-rich environment of digestion, and it can change how the compound interacts with those efflux pumps. Preclinical research on berberine phospholipid complexes and liposomal forms has reported improved absorption compared with plain berberine.

The honest caveat

A good deal of the delivery research on berberine is preclinical or in animal models, and human data on liposomal berberine specifically is still developing. As with any liposomal product, the quality of the formulation matters enormously. We think the mechanism is sound and the direction is right, and we would rather tell you that plainly than overstate the evidence.

Absorption is necessary, but it is not the whole story. Frequently asked questions

Is liposomal berberine better than regular berberine?
Regular berberine is very poorly absorbed, so delivery systems that improve its bioavailability, including liposomal and phospholipid forms, are designed to get more of it into circulation. The size of the benefit depends on the quality of the specific formulation.
Why does berberine cause stomach upset for some people?
Because so little raw berberine is absorbed, a large amount stays in the gut, where it can cause cramping or changes in digestion for some people. Encapsulating it in a phospholipid shell is one way formulators aim to reduce that discomfort.
When should I take berberine?
Berberine is commonly taken with meals. The Metabolic splits its daily amount into an AM and a PM capsule taken with food, which is intended to support steadier levels across the day.
