Quality and Trust

How to trust a supplement: third-party testing, cGMP, and COAs

Supplements are not approved by the FDA before they are sold. That surprises most people, and it is the single most important fact for understanding quality. Here is how good brands actually prove what is in the bottle, and how you can check.

Reviewed by the Prickless Bio Science Team Updated August 2026 7 min read

If you assume a supplement on a store shelf has been tested and approved by a government agency the way a prescription drug has, you are in good company, and you are also mistaken. The rules are different, and knowing how they actually work is the best defense you have as a shopper. The good news is that the tools to prove quality exist and are not that complicated once someone explains them plainly.

Key takeaways

  • Supplements are regulated as food under the 1994 DSHEA law and are not pre-approved by the FDA.
  • Manufacturers must follow cGMP rules (21 CFR Part 111), but that is a floor, not a guarantee of independent testing.
  • Independent analyses have repeatedly found products that do not match their labels.
  • Third-party testing checks identity, potency, and contaminants like heavy metals and microbes.
  • A Certificate of Analysis (COA) documents those results for a specific batch.

How supplements are actually regulated

In the United States, dietary supplements are governed by the Dietary Supplement Health and Education Act of 1994, known as DSHEA. Under this law, supplements are treated as a category of food rather than as drugs. That means a company does not submit a product to the FDA for approval before selling it. Instead, the manufacturer is legally responsible for making sure the product is safe and that the label is truthful, and the FDA mostly steps in after the fact if there is a problem.

There is a manufacturing standard that applies. The FDA's current Good Manufacturing Practices for supplements, written in 21 CFR Part 111, set rules for cleanliness, record keeping, and process control in the facility. Following cGMP is required and it matters, but notice what it is: a set of rules about how a product is made. It is a floor. It does not by itself prove that an independent lab confirmed the finished product matches its label.

Why this leaves room for bad products

Because oversight is mostly post-market, the marketplace has real variability. Independent testing organizations and academic researchers have, over many years, found supplements that contained less active ingredient than promised, more than promised, the wrong ingredient, or unwanted contaminants. The pharmacologist Pieter Cohen and colleagues have published repeatedly in medical journals documenting mislabeled and adulterated products across categories. None of this means supplements are bad. It means the label alone is not proof, and you should look for evidence behind it.

Botanical ingredients deserve special mention. Because plants draw up whatever is in their soil and water, botanical raw materials can carry trace heavy metals such as lead, arsenic, cadmium, and mercury. A responsible botanical supplement is tested to confirm those contaminants are within recognized safe limits.

Our honest read

We are not going to pretend the whole industry is a scam, because it is not. But we also will not pretend a pretty label is proof of anything. The honest position is that quality has to be demonstrated with independent testing, batch by batch, and any brand asking for your trust should be willing to show its work.

What third-party testing actually checks

Third-party testing means a laboratory with no financial stake in the outcome, separate from the manufacturer, tests the product. A thorough test covers a few distinct things:

  • Identity. Is the ingredient actually what it claims to be, for example real berberine and not a look-alike?
  • Potency, or assay. Does the amount of active ingredient match the label, within tight tolerances?
  • Heavy metals. Are lead, arsenic, cadmium, and mercury below safe thresholds?
  • Microbial safety. Is the product free of harmful bacteria, yeast, and mold?

You may also see certifications from independent programs such as USP Verified or NSF, which layer an audited standard on top of testing. And the document that ties it all together is the Certificate of Analysis, or COA, a report of the test results for a specific batch. If a brand can hand you a COA for the batch you are holding, that is the difference between a claim and a receipt.

Frequently asked questions

Are supplements tested by the FDA before they are sold?
No. Under the 1994 DSHEA law, dietary supplements are regulated as a category of food and are not approved by the FDA before sale. Manufacturers are responsible for safety and honest labeling, and the FDA acts mainly after a product is already on the market. That is exactly why independent third-party testing matters.
What does third-party tested mean?
It means an independent laboratory, separate from the manufacturer, has tested the product to confirm its identity, that the amount of active ingredient matches the label, and that contaminants such as heavy metals and microbes are within safe limits. The results are documented in a Certificate of Analysis.
What is a Certificate of Analysis?
A Certificate of Analysis, or COA, is a lab document that reports the test results for a specific batch of product, including identity, potency, and contaminant testing. Reputable brands can provide a COA for the batch you are taking.
